\begin{abstract}
AI resembles the abacus more than it resembles writing. The abacus gave humanity a shared
capacity to compute and a shared way to check computations. Writing gave something
different: a durable record, a way to send results to people one never meets, and therefore
cumulative progress. Mathematics has already lived through the transition this paper is
concerned with. Once proofs exceeded what humans could check, the field adopted proof
assistants such as Lean, and libraries such as Mathlib became a shared, machine-checked
record that people and programs extend together.

We present \textsc{Hakem} (Turkish: \emph{referee}), an attempt to build the corresponding
``writing'' for AI beyond mathematics. Hakem is a small language in which models write
theories over typed vocabularies (each symbol definitional, empirical or normative),
glossaries between disciplines, claims (lemmas, analogies, metaphor refutations, policy
verdicts) and certificates (natural-deduction proofs and finite counter-models). A checker
accepts or rejects each document; its soundness is proved once, in the Lean~4 kernel, for
every possible document. Accepted lemmas are reused by later proofs. A machine-readable
verdict and a ledger turn acceptance into a verifiable reward signal, automating the part of
the oversight of autonomous learning that needs no human in the loop.

The kernel carries three principles as general theorems: a result carries to another
discipline only along a structure-preserving map; a structure-breaking map is a metaphor,
refutable by a concrete counter-model; and no policy conclusion follows from premises
without normative symbols (Hume's law). All 21 claims of the current release receive formal
acceptance from a judge that replays them through six independent kernels in a sandbox.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\emph{Convention.} A claim marked \ok{} refers to a named Lean theorem that receives
formal acceptance in the repository's \texttt{KARAR.md}. Section~\ref{sec:accept} states
exactly what that acceptance means. The title pays homage to~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.

\section{Vision}

\paragraph{The abacus and writing.}
The abacus is shared computational capacity plus a shared verification mechanism: anyone
can recompute and see whether the beads agree. Today's AI systems are abacus-like in this
sense: enormous shared capacity, and verification that is local and ad hoc. What writing
added was not more computation. It added a \emph{record} (results outlive the computation
that produced them), \emph{communication} (results move between parties who never meet) and
\emph{accumulation} (new results are built on recorded ones). Our thesis is that AI needs
the same layer: a common written form in which models record what they have established,
send it to other models and build on it, judged by a referee that anyone can re-run.

\paragraph{Mathematics as the precedent.}
Mathematics already crossed the threshold where computation exceeds human checking. The
response was not to trust the computation. It was to write results in a formal language,
check them with a small trusted kernel~\cite{demoura2021lean4} and accumulate them in a shared
library~\cite{mathlib2020}. Hakem imitates the loop of conjecture, machine-checked proof,
library and new conjecture, and extends it to claims that cross disciplines.

\paragraph{Design principles.}
\emph{De Bruijn criterion}~\cite{barendregt2005challenge}: anyone, human or AI, may propose;
the proposer is an untrusted prover; judgment belongs to a small, auditable kernel.
\emph{Independent refereeing}: the trusted part should be small enough to re-implement.
Hakem reuses Lean's kernel, which has several independent implementations~\cite{carneiro2024lean4lean},
and keeps its own trusted vocabulary small. \emph{Chaos is located, not removed}:
measurement and value choices are outside the kernel's jurisdiction, but they must appear
explicitly, as symbol roles, and results are conditional on them. \emph{The word ``proven''
belongs to the judge}: only the judge writes formal acceptance, for a specific formal
statement; natural-language readings are shown separately and marked unchecked.

\section{Oversight in autonomous learning}

Systems that improve themselves need a signal that tells good outputs from bad ones. When
that signal comes from a learned model, it can be gamed. When it comes from a checker whose
soundness is a theorem, the only remaining question is whether the \emph{statement} means
what we intend. Training provers against formal checkers is established in
mathematics~\cite{yang2023leandojo}; Hakem makes such a checker available for a wider class
of claims: \emph{analogies} (``this result of discipline A holds in B under this glossary''),
\emph{metaphors} (``this glossary breaks structure; here is a world of B that refutes it'') and
\emph{policy verdicts} (``under these explicitly normative premises this policy follows, and
the facts alone do not decide it'').

The loop is implemented without a human in it. Proposer models write candidate documents to
\texttt{inbox/}. \texttt{hakem json} produces a one-line verdict. Accepted documents move to
the corpus under their SHA-256 content hash; every verdict is appended to
\texttt{ledger.jsonl} with a 0/1 reward and per-claim detail. Later documents reuse accepted
lemmas, and \texttt{tools/corpus.py} turns the corpus into kernel certificates.

\section{The logic}

Disciplines are data. A \emph{signature} lists function and relation symbols with arities and
\emph{roles} (\texttt{tanim} definitional, \texttt{ampirik} empirical, \texttt{normatif}
normative). Formulas are first-order with equality, with de Bruijn indices. A
\emph{structure} gives a carrier and interpretations; a \emph{theory} is a list of axioms,
optionally with a class of admissible structures.

\paragraph{Analogy as signature morphism.}
A glossary $\sigma$ sends each source symbol to a target term or formula (e.g.\ ``energy
$\mapsto$ deposits $-$ loans''). A target world $M$ is read in the source language by the
\emph{reduct} $\sigma(M)$, in the spirit of institution theory~\cite{goguen1992institutions}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \thm{satisfaction}\ok: $M \models \sigma(\varphi) \iff \sigma(M) \models \varphi$.
\item An analogy certificate requires that $B$ entail the translation of every axiom of $A$,
  and that $B$ have a model (non-vacuity).
\item \thm{transfer}\ok: $A \models \varphi \Rightarrow B \models \sigma(\varphi)$.
  \thm{compose\_exists}\ok: analogies compose. \thm{source\_consistent}\ok: if an analogy
  exists, the source has a model.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metaphor certificate.}
A source theorem $\varphi$ and a target model in which $\sigma(\varphi)$ fails.
\thm{refutation\_sound}\ok: such a certificate excludes \emph{every} analogy that agrees with
$\sigma$ on the symbols the certificate anchors.

\paragraph{Fact and value.}
\texttt{reinterp} changes the interpretations of normative symbols only. A \emph{policy
verdict} for $\varphi$ in $T$ consists of $T \models \varphi$, a world satisfying $T$, and an
alternative differing from it only in normative symbols, in which $\varphi$ fails.
\thm{hume\_law}\ok: if every axiom of $T$ is descriptive, no policy verdict exists; this is a
formal version of the is--ought gap~\cite{hume1739treatise,schurz1997isought}.
\thm{verdict\_same\_facts}\ok: in every verdict the two worlds agree on all descriptive
symbols, and the conclusion is true in one and false in the other. Whether a formula is
normative is \emph{computed} from its symbols, never declared.

\section{Certificates are data}

\texttt{Proof} is a term language for natural deduction (with equality and excluded middle);
\texttt{infer} computes what a proof proves. \thm{calculus\_sound}\ok:
$\texttt{check}\ T.\texttt{axioms}\ \varphi\ p = \texttt{true} \Rightarrow T \models \varphi$.
\texttt{FinStructure} has carrier $\mathrm{Fin}(n{+}1)$ and computable interpretations;
\thm{finite\_sat\_iff}\ok, \thm{finite\_models\_sound}\ok, \thm{finite\_refutes\_sound}\ok{}
state that on finite structures computation agrees with truth for closed formulas, and
\thm{refutation\_of\_data}\ok, \thm{verdict\_of\_data}\ok{} build certificates entirely from
data. For standard mathematics, admissible models of a theory with background are those whose
standard part is isomorphic to a fixed structure such as $\mathbb{N}$ or $\mathbb{Z}$;
\thm{iso\_sat}\ok, \thm{background\_sound}\ok, \thm{calculus\_sound\_bg}\ok{} and
\thm{entails\_of\_std}\ok{} allow reasoning directly on the standard carrier. Once these are
accepted, a certificate is checked by kernel computation alone (\texttt{decide +kernel}); no
tactic, parser or compiler is involved.

\section{The language}

A Hakem document is a list of S-expressions (specification: \texttt{DIL.md}):
\begin{lstlisting}
(hakem 1)
(sig phys (fn E 1 ampirik) (rel Exch 2 tanim))
(theory Physics phys (ax cons (all (all (imp (Exch v0 v1) (= (E v0) (E v1)))))))
(sig bank (fn Dep 1 ampirik) (fn Net 1 ampirik) (rel Tr 2 tanim) (rel Cr 2 tanim))
(theory Banking bank (ax trNet ...) (ax crNet ...) (ax crMoney ...) ...)
(lemma stepNet Banking (all (all (imp (or (Tr v0 v1) (Cr v0 v1)) (= (Net v0) (Net v1))))) PROOF)
(morph net phys bank (fn E (Net p0)) (rel Exch (or (Tr p0 p1) (Cr p0 p1))))
(analogy physicsToBanking Physics Banking net (model 2 ...) (oblig cons (ax stepNet)))
(refute depositsMetaphor Physics Banking dep (all ...) (ax cons) (model 2 ...))
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{Doc.HoldsTree} states that every claim of a document holds.
\thm{checkTree\_sound}\ok{} and \thm{checkDoc\_sound}\ok{} state that every document the
checker accepts means what it says: the fundamental theorem of the language, proved once for
all documents.

\paragraph{Trust chain.}
Text $\to$ tree $\to$ claims $\to$ verdict. Only the first step, a reader of about 40 lines,
runs outside the kernel; anyone can re-implement it and compare trees. We measured kernel
reduction of \texttt{String.toList} at roughly 0.1\,s per character, whereas checking a
document's tree takes about 2\,s per document; this motivated certifying from the tree.

\paragraph{Corpus.}
\thm{korunum\_holds}\ok{} and \thm{politika\_holds}\ok{} certify two conformance documents:
energy conservation transfers to banking under ``energy $\mapsto$ net position''; the glossary
``energy $\mapsto$ deposits'' is refuted (credit creates money); and a policy verdict whose
normative premise is computed as normative. Three bad documents are rejected: a wrong proof,
a normative symbol mislabelled as empirical, and a ``counterexample'' that is not a model.

\section{What formal acceptance means}\label{sec:accept}

The judge accepts a claim only if (1) Lean's kernel accepts it and the export is re-checked by
the independent Rust kernel nanoda; (2) the statement in the trusted \texttt{Challenge.lean}
and the untrusted \texttt{Solution} are identical \emph{as terms}; (3) every definition the
statement depends on (its \emph{meaning base}) is identical on both sides; (4) the proof uses
only \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Quot.sound}, \texttt{Classical.choice}. In continuous
integration, the Lean FRO's \texttt{lake comparator --paranoid} additionally replays the
solution in a sandbox; in its first run all six kernels accepted (Lean paranoid,
lean4lean~\cite{carneiro2024lean4lean}, nanoda, con-leche, con-ron, Lean default).
Current state: 21/21 claims accepted.

Acceptance does \emph{not} establish that a reading describes its statement correctly, that a
model is faithful to the real discipline, or that measured values or value choices are right.

\section{Adversarial validation}

A self-test submits attack-shaped solutions; each must be rejected for the expected reason: a
new axiom; \texttt{sorry}; the same name with a different statement; a hollowed-out
redefinition of trusted data under the same names; \texttt{native\_decide}; and the three bad
documents. Validation found and fixed two real defects in our judge. First, the axiom
collector silently returned nothing, because \texttt{ConstantInfo.value?} omits theorem
bodies, so a \texttt{sorry} proof would have passed. Second, the meaning base counted only some
modules, so a hollowed-out corpus tree was accepted. We report these because they are exactly
the failure mode a referee must anticipate.

\section{Related work}

Lean~4~\cite{demoura2021lean4} and Mathlib~\cite{mathlib2020} are the precedent and the
substrate. Metamath~\cite{megill2019metamath} and the de Bruijn
criterion~\cite{barendregt2005challenge} motivate a small re-implementable checker.
Institution theory~\cite{goguen1992institutions} supplies the satisfaction condition and
theory morphisms. Ologs~\cite{spivak2012ologs} and categorical tools for scientific modelling
suggest the natural long-term formulation of analogies as model morphisms. Learning to prove
against formal checkers~\cite{yang2023leandojo} is the setting of verifiable rewards that
Hakem extends beyond mathematics.

\section{Limitations and future work}

\emph{Faithfulness}: whether a theory faithfully represents a discipline cannot be checked by
Lean; we propose plural, independent human review pinned to definition fingerprints.
\emph{Cross-document imports}: version~1 reuses lemmas only within a document; next are
imports by content hash, turning the corpus into a library. \emph{Backgrounds in the
language}: the kernel supports $\mathbb{N}/\mathbb{Z}$ backgrounds, the language not yet;
$\mathbb{R}$ and analysis need Mathlib. \emph{Proof search}: the language carries proofs and
does not find them; a search tool needs no trust. \emph{Independent implementations}: a second
reader and checker written against the specification and conformance corpus.

\section{Conclusion}

Hakem is a small step toward a common written form for machine reasoning: one in which models
record, communicate and build on interdisciplinary results, judged by a referee whose
soundness is a theorem and which remains small enough to rebuild.
